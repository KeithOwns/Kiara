Subject: Kiara (“Kiki”), small adult female dog, approximately 11.7 lb.

1. Abdominal, Mammary, and Reproductive Appearance

Nipples / Mammary Tissue

Photos of Kiara’s underside showed fairly prominent nipples with some variation in size and color. A few nipple tips appeared mildly dry or keratinized, with a little superficial debris or crusting nearby. No obvious severe redness, ulceration, discharge, or generalized mammary swelling was visible.

Prominent or slightly elongated nipples can be normal. They can also occur after prior pregnancy or nursing, but nipple appearance alone cannot prove whether Kiara has ever had puppies.

  • Monitor for firm or pea-sized lumps.
  • Watch for thickening, pain, heat, swelling, discharge, bleeding, or rapid change.

Blue Line Tattoo

The faint blue-green line on Kiara’s belly was considered very likely to be a spay tattoo. Shelters and veterinarians commonly place a small green or blue abdominal tattoo at sterilization so future caregivers can tell that a dog has been spayed. It appeared flat and healed rather than inflamed. Shelter records can confirm the procedure.

Vulva / External Reproductive Organ

Kiara’s vulva appeared relatively small, closed, and possibly mildly recessed or hooded by surrounding skin. It did not look obviously enlarged, prolapsed, infected, or otherwise abnormal in the photos.

The size was considered within a normal range for a small adult female dog, especially a spayed dog. Normal appearance varies with age, hormones, body shape, spay status, and heat cycles.

Pregnancy and giving birth can temporarily enlarge and stretch a dog’s vulva. It usually becomes smaller again after recovery, although some dogs may remain a little larger or looser. Vulva size alone cannot reliably show whether a dog has previously given birth.

  • Seek veterinary attention for foul odor or abnormal discharge.
  • Also watch for persistent licking, painful or difficult urination, urinary accidents, marked redness or swelling, bleeding, fever, or lethargy.

2. Possible Dandruff Near the Base of the Tail

You noticed possible flaking near Kiara’s tail base and asked whether two human hair products could be used on her. The recommendation was not to use the human shampoo or conditioner on Kiara. Human hair products are formulated for human scalp chemistry and may contain fragrance or cleansing agents that irritate canine skin; the shampoo discussed also contained sodium salicylate.

  • Check carefully for fleas or black, pepper-like flea dirt.
  • Brush gently and avoid frequent bathing.
  • If bathing is needed, use a dog-formulated fragrance-free hypoallergenic or oatmeal shampoo and rinse thoroughly.
  • See a veterinarian if the area becomes red, greasy, smelly, itchy, painful, or develops hair loss.

3. Human Conditioner as a Leave-In Product

You asked whether the conditioner could be used as a leave-in conditioner on your own hair. The answer was yes, in a very small amount, even though it is formulated as a rinse-out product.

  • Use about a pea-sized amount or less.
  • Rub between wet hands and apply mainly to damp mid-lengths and ends.
  • Avoid the scalp if fragrance causes irritation.
  • Reduce the amount if hair becomes greasy, heavy, or waxy.

4. Dachshund Background

Dachshunds were originally bred in Germany primarily to hunt badgers underground. “Dachshund” literally means “badger dog.” Their long bodies, short strong legs, digging ability, deep chests, and loud bark helped them work in burrows and alert hunters. They were also used for rabbits, foxes, and tracking wounded game.

5. Rough Paw Pads

You reported that the skin on the bottoms of Kiara’s toes and paws felt rough in some areas. Mild roughness can be normal because paw pads naturally become thicker and tougher from walking on pavement, dry ground, and rough surfaces.

  • Watch for cracks, bleeding, redness, swelling, pain, limping, excessive licking, thick “hairy” keratin buildup, odor, or discharge.
  • For mild dryness, a thin layer of dog-safe paw balm was suggested.
  • Avoid human foot creams, particularly products containing zinc oxide, salicylic acid, urea, essential oils, or strong fragrance.

6. DNA Breed Testing

Least-Expensive Approach

The least expensive reliable approach discussed was to wait for a sale on a breed-only cheek-swab test. Historically, lower-cost options include Wisdom Panel or Embark when discounted. Truly free commercial dog DNA tests are uncommon.

  • Ask West Valley Animal Shelter about donated kits, adopter promotions, or manufacturer partnerships.
  • Look for veterinary-school or canine-genetics research studies.
  • Watch for pet expos, rescue events, promotions, and giveaways.
  • Only use an unused, unregistered secondhand kit.

Photo-based breed-identifier apps were distinguished from true DNA testing and considered much less reliable.

Potential Benefits

  • More informed breed ancestry estimates than appearance alone.
  • Possible inherited-disease risk information with health-panel tests.
  • Context for coat, behavior, prey drive, digging, barking, or body shape.
  • Possible medication-related genetic variants.
  • Potential identification of genetic relatives in a company database.

Limitations were emphasized: breed percentages are estimates, companies may disagree, a genetic risk does not guarantee disease, and a clear result does not guarantee lifelong health.

Final Decision

You decided not to have Kiki DNA tested, concluding that the results were unlikely to change her most important preventive care.

8. Vet Advice About Jumping

You reported that Kiara’s veterinarian asked you to make sure she does not jump down from surfaces that are too high. That advice was considered reasonable, particularly for a small dog with a potentially long-backed build or possible Dachshund ancestry.

  • Use a non-slip ramp or sturdy pet steps for furniture and cars.
  • Discourage jumping off beds, couches, and other elevated surfaces.
  • When lifting, support both the chest and hindquarters.
  • Keep her lean to reduce unnecessary load on the back and joints.

Urgent warning signs include sudden back pain, yelping, reluctance to move, wobbling, dragging a leg, marked weakness, or loss of bladder control.

9. Running With Kiara

Running with Kiara may be reasonable, but a conservative approach was recommended because of her small size, possible long-backed conformation, and the veterinarian’s warning about jumping.

  • Begin with brisk walks.
  • Add short jog intervals, such as 1–2 minutes at a time.
  • Increase distance and duration gradually.
  • Prefer level terrain and use a well-fitted harness.
  • Avoid repeated jumping, hard sprints, steep downhill running, long runs on concrete, hot weather, and hot pavement.

Stop if she lags, limps, bunny-hops, changes gait, yelps, repeatedly sits, seems stiff afterward, or resists being picked up. Before making her a regular running partner, it was suggested that you ask her veterinarian whether any back, hip, knee, or cardiac issue would limit jogging.

10. Main Preventive-Care Priorities Going Forward

After deciding against DNA testing, you identified the main medical issues you want to monitor: teeth, weight, and preventing high jumps. Those were affirmed as sensible priorities.

  • Dental care: brush with dog toothpaste and watch for tartar, bad breath, red gums, loose teeth, or trouble chewing.
  • Weight: keep her lean enough that the ribs are easy to feel without being sharply visible.
  • Back protection: ramps/steps, careful lifting, and avoiding high-impact jumps.
  • Ears: because brown material had previously been noticed, watch for odor, redness, scratching, head shaking, or discharge.
  • Routine veterinary care: vaccinations, parasite prevention, nail care, and periodic physical examinations.

Overall Takeaway

Nothing in the abdominal or reproductive photos appeared obviously emergent. Kiara’s vulva looked within a normal range, the blue abdominal line was most consistent with a spay tattoo, and the nipples looked prominent but without obvious severe inflammation. Her practical health priorities are maintaining good dental health and a lean body condition, protecting her back and joints from unnecessary impact, monitoring ears and skin, and using gradual low-impact exercise. A DNA test is optional and not necessary for providing these preventive measures.
